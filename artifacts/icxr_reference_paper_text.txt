 A B S T R A C T
Redirected walking (RDW) is a virtual reality locomotion technique that enables users to explore large virtual 
environments within a limited physical space. While state-of-the-art methods based on physical trajectory 
planning make effective use of physical space, some of them often compromise user comfort due to frequent 
directional reversals in curvature gain. To address this, this paper proposes a novel RDW method that 
integrates strafing gain with pose score guidance. Our approach discretizes the physical space into a series 
of standard poses, each with a long-term safety score, and redirects the user toward the optimal pose. The 
main contribution is a path generation algorithm that decomposes redirection into two sequential stages to 
ensure stable gains for each planned path: it first uses the curvature gain to steer the user along an arc for 
orientation alignment, and then inserts a straight path segment with constant strafing gain to achieve positional 
alignment with the target pose. Simulation experiments demonstrate a reduction in resets, while the user study 
shows lower Simulator Sickness Questionnaire scores compared to previous methods. Our work explores the 
potential of combining novel gains with state-of-the-art methods to create a more effective and comfortable 
RDW controller algorithm.
1. Introduction
Virtual Reality (VR) technology provides immersive and realistic 
content, owing to significant advancements in technologies such as 
three-dimensional content generation and wearable interactive devices. 
It is now widely integrated into a variety of domains, including ed-
ucation, healthcare, and entertainment. However, a major challenge 
for VR technology is overcoming the conflict between a vast virtual 
environment (VE) and the limited physical space (PE). To this end, a 
variety of virtual locomotion techniques have been proposed, such as 
teleportation [1], walking-in-place [2,3], omnidirectional treadmill [4], 
and Redirected Walking (RDW) [5,6]. Among these, RDW holds a 
unique advantage as it enables users to walk naturally, providing a 
higher sense of immersion and spatial presence.
Redirected Walking leverages the complexities of the human percep-
tual system to apply imperceptible manipulations to the virtual scene. 
This remapping of physical walking to virtual movement is known as

Consequently, it is logical to explore whether the user’s walking 
path can be adjusted without frequently steering their turn. A potential 
solution is strafing gain [12], a novel type of gain that applies a subtle 
lateral displacement as the user walks forward, causing them to move 
along a diagonal path while maintaining their intended orientation. 
However, effective integration of strafing gain into advanced RDW 
controllers remains an underexplored problem.
This paper proposes a novel RDW method that incorporates strafing 
gain to address a specific issue in existing trajectory planning methods, 
where curvature gain frequently switches between opposite directions, 
which can be more perceptible to users and potentially lead to dis-
comfort. We adopt the method of optimal pose guidance, discretizing 
the physical space into a series of standard poses, each comprising a 
position and an orientation, and pre-calculating a score for each pose 
that represents its long-term safety. With the highest-scoring pose set 
as the target, we introduce an innovative path generation algorithm 
to redirect the user. This algorithm decomposes the complex task of 
pose alignment into two distinct stages: first, it utilizes the maximum 
curvature gain to guide the user along a single circular arc to achieve 
orientation alignment. Subsequently, at a suitable tangent point on the 
arc, it inserts a straight path applied constant strafing gain to align the 
user’s position. This design ensures that the direction of applied gains 
remains stable within a single guidance maneuver, thereby effectively 
decreasing the number of resets while significantly enhancing user 
comfort.
The main contributions of the paper are as follows:
1. We propose a novel path generation algorithm that combines 
curvature and strafing gains to stably redirect the user to the 
target pose.
2. Based on this algorithm, we implement a novel RDW controller. 
To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first to integrate 
strafing gain into RDW controllers.
3. The results of simulation experiments demonstrate that our 
method reduces the number of resets, while the user study shows 
that it improves user comfort with lower SSQ scores.
2. Related work
2.1. Redirection gains
Redirected Walking [5] was first proposed by Razzaque et al. in 
2001. It leverages the complexities of the human perceptual system 
to apply imperceptible manipulations to the virtual scene, thereby 
remapping the relationship between physical and virtual movements. 
This technique allows users to experience large-scale virtual locomotion 
within a limited physical space. The mapping between physical and 
virtual motion is defined by redirection gains, which were initially 
classified by Steinicke et al. [6] into three categories: translation gain 
scales the user’s virtual movement, rotation gain alters the angle of the 
user rotations, and curvature gain bends straight paths into curves.
Subsequent research has defined and measured more novel types 
of gains. These include bending gain [13], a variant of curvature 
gain; deviation gain [14], which converts stationary rotations into 
translational shifts; bidirectional rotation difference (BiRD) gain [15], 
which uses the user back-and-forth head movements to deflect their 
path. Furthermore, research has extended into the vertical dimension, 
introducing slope gain [16,17], vertical gain [18], and jumping gain 
that manipulate jumping actions.
Early research generally avoided applying gains during non-forward 
movements like sideward walking. However, the work of Cho et al. [19] 
revealed that users are actually less sensitive to gains during non-
forward motion, which results in wider perceptual thresholds. Build-
ing on this, Dong et al. [20] developed FREE-RDW, a method that 
integrates these thresholds into a multi-user collision avoidance strat-
egy. Collectively, these studies explored the potential of lateral mo-
tion, establishing its viability for enhancing RDW without significantly 
increasing simulator sickness.
While the aforementioned studies apply gains to a user’s lateral 
movements, the strafing gain proposed by You et al. [12] explores 
a different but related concept. It applies a lateral displacement to a 
forward-walking user, causing them to move along a diagonal path in 
the physical world while their virtual path remains straight (Fig.  1a). 
Unlike curvature gains that induce rotation, their study confirmed that 
users maintain their heading and respond to the lateral shift with a 
diagonal path, rather than a rotational adjustment. This characteristic 
gives strafing gain significant potential in various scenarios, such as 
subtly adjusting the user’s physical position to avoid obstacles, com-
bining with other gains for complex path planning, or assisting in 
aligning physical and virtual environments. However, although the 
perceptual thresholds for strafing gain have been measured, its effective 
integration into advanced RDW controllers remains a direction that 
requires further exploration.
2.2. RDW controller algorithms
Recent advances in RDW have pushed the field toward several 
challenging directions, aiming to overcome physical space limitations 
while providing more natural virtual experiences. As summarized in a 
recent comprehensive survey by Liu et al. [21], these directions include 
the development of more sophisticated controller algorithms, the cre-
ation of coordination strategies to support multi-user environments, the 
integration of multisensory cues, and the design of methods capable of 
adapting to dynamic physical spaces and obstacles, among others.
Among these, to effectively manage redirection gains and prevent 
users from colliding with physical boundaries, researchers have pro-
posed a series of RDW algorithms. Razzaque proposed several al-
gorithms in his pioneering work, including S2C, S2O, and Steer-to-
Multiple-Targets (S2MT) [7], designed to guide users toward specific 
targets or regions within the physical space to minimize collisions. 
To handle more complex physical environments, the APF method is 
introduced to RDW, placing the user in a virtual force field of attractive 
and repulsive forces to guide them away from obstacles and toward 
open areas [8–10]. More recently, Chen et al. introduced the APF 
Steer-to-Target (APF-S2T) [22] to address the problem of traditional 
APF vectors in narrow corridors being directed toward opposing walls, 
instead guiding users toward more open spaces.
In recent years, Reinforcement Learning (RL) has provided a new 
direction for RDW algorithm design, differing from traditional rule-
based methods. For instance, the Steer-to-Optimal-Target (S2OT) [23] 
method by Lee et al. uses RL to determine the optimal steering target; 
the Steering via Reinforcement Learning (SRL) [24] method by Strauss 
et al. utilizes RL to directly predict redirection gain values; and the 
Selective Redirection Controller (SRC) [25] by Lee et al. employs RL to 
dynamically switch between different controllers.
Concurrently, advanced methods based on physical trajectory plan-
ning have also been developed, which demonstrate superior perfor-
mance in complex environments by evaluating the reachability of 
physical paths to optimize redirection decisions.
2.3. Physical trajectory planning for RDW
RDW algorithms based on physical trajectory planning address 
many constraints that previous methods could not handle by modeling 
and evaluating future physical paths. Xu et al. proposed optimal pose 
guided [11], which discretizes the physical space into a series of 
standard poses containing both position and orientation. It scores these

in redirection methods that use optimal pose guidance. Strafing gain 
could offer new possibilities for both algorithmic performance and user 
experience by subtly adjusting users physical position while maintain-
ing their intended orientation. Therefore, this paper proposes a novel 
path generation algorithm that integrates strafing gain into physical 
trajectory planning, aiming to explore this potential to further enhance 
the performance and experience of RDW controllers.

smooth, collision-free path that satisfies the gain thresholds. To ensure 
the redirection remains imperceptible to users, our path generation 
algorithm applies only one of the two types of gain (curvature or 
strafing) one at a time.

3.3. RDW controller strategy
At runtime, our primary goal is to guide the user toward a standard 
pose with the highest long-term safety score. When the user walks 
forward, the controller first selects an accessible standard pose with 
the highest score as the target and generates a guidance path based 
on the algorithm in Section 3.2. For implementation purposes, this 
path is discretized into a set of path points at 0.1-meter intervals, 
where each point contains information on position, orientation, and the 
corresponding gain (i.e., whether to apply curvature or strafing gain, 
and its direction and value). During locomotion, in each frame, the 
controller matches the user current state to the nearest point on the 
path and applies the gain associated with that point, thereby ensuring 
the user follows the planned path under the gain constraints. To timely 
adjust the guidance path, we set thresholds. When the user’s distance 
or orientation relative to the nearest point exceeds the thresholds, we 
consider the user rotates or deviates from the path, and the controller 
re-selects a target pose and generates a new path.

4. Simulation experiment
4.1. Simulation design
Although research [30] indicates that generated paths may not 
accurately reflect the natural walking patterns of real users, simulation 
is still considered an effective way to evaluate RDW methods [31], 
as it allows a large number of tests to be conducted in various envi-
ronments. As a preliminary evaluation of our proposed algorithm, we 
first conducted a series of simulation experiments with the OpenRDW 
platform [32]. We did not make any modifications to the source code 
of the existing baseline methods provided within the platform and 
implemented our algorithm as a new redirector by extending the base 
Redirector class. All experiments used the default gain parameters.
In the simulation experiments, a simulated user walks along prede-
fined virtual paths. Upon reaching a waypoint, the user turns to face 
the next waypoint and then walks straight toward it. We generate 100 
independent random virtual paths, each with a total length of 400 m. 
Waypoints are randomly generated within a range of 5 to 15 m from 
the previous one. To ensure fairness, all algorithms are tested on the 
same set of virtual paths.
We evaluated the RDW algorithms in three physical environments 
with different obstacle layouts, as shown in Fig.  3, representing a range 
of application scenarios from simple to complex. The obstacle-free Envi-
ronment A represents the most common and ideal layout, applicable to 
scenarios where users have a clear, open tracking space. Environments 
B and C simulate some indoor physical settings, such as a furnished 
room, in order to test an algorithm’s performance in reducing the 
spatial restrictions imposed on the user. The motivation for including 
these layouts is to explore the potential of RDW when a large, clear area 
is not available. Furthermore, this set of physical layouts is similar to 
prior work, allowing for a fair and direct comparison. All three layouts 
are tested in two sizes of square physical environments: 10 m × 10 m 
and 15 m × 15 m, resulting in a total of six simulated PEs. We set the 
virtual space as an obstacle-free open area, allowing the simulated user 
to walk freely within it.

4.2. Results and discussion
Fig.  4 presents a performance comparison of our algorithm (Ours) 
with five state-of-the-art RDW methods including S2C [7], S2O [7], 
TAPF [10], SRL [24], and OPG [11], across three physical environments 
(Environments A, B, and C) and two sizes (10 m × 10 m and 15 m 
× 15 m). For methods lacking a reset strategy, such as S2C and S2O, 
we employ two-one-turn as the default reset strategy. We analyzed the 
number of resets and average virtual distance between resets in all six 
environments.
As Shapiro–Wilk tests showed that not all results followed normal 
distribution, we employed the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis H test to 
analyze the overall differences among the algorithm groups, followed 
by Dunn’s post hoc test with Bonferroni correction to specifically com-
pare the performance between our method and the others. Statistical 
analysis revealed statistically significant differences in the number of 
resets and average virtual distance between resets among the algo-
rithms in all six environments (𝑝 < . 001). Post hoc pairwise comparisons 
between our method and other methods are shown in Tables  1 and
2. Statistically, our algorithm demonstrated a significant improvement 
over all other algorithms except OPG in most tested environments. The 
only non-significant comparisons are with TAPF in Environment A at 
both 10 m × 10 m, and with S2O in Environment B at 15 m × 15 m. 
Compared to OPG, our algorithm was better at reducing resets in four 
environments except for the 10 m × 10 m Environment A and 15 m ×
15 m Environment B.

tages of our method and the specific applicable scenarios for different

5. User study
5.1. User study design
To further evaluate the effectiveness and subjective comfort of 
our method, we conducted a user study following the simulation ex-
periments. We recruited 23 participants (13 male, 10 female, aged 
22–26), all of whom were right-handed, with 17 stating they had VR 
experiences. The experiment used an HTC VIVE Focus Vision head-
mounted display (HMD), connected via wireless streaming to a laptop 
equipped with an AMD Ryzen 7 5800H processor, 16 GB of RAM, 
and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU. Similar to the simulation, we 
conducted user study experiments using OpenRDW.
To ensure a stable and low-latency wireless stream between the 
HMD and the host computer, the physical tracking space is a 7 m × 7 m 
area. While the layout is a simplified version of Environment C with a 
0.5 m buffer zone as shown in Fig.  5b, no actual obstacles were placed 
in the area to ensure the safety of participants. The virtual scene is an 
open forest environment (Fig.  5a), following the experimental setup of 
previous work [29]. The participants task is to collect orange spheres,

automatically recorded the number of resets and the average virtual 
distance between resets. After all three sessions were completed, a 
brief interview was conducted to collect subjective feedback on walking 
naturalness and comfort. The entire experiment lasted approximately 
40 min per participant.

5.2. Results
Fig.  6 shows a comparison of our method with TAPF and OPG on the 
number of resets, the average distance between resets, and SSQ scores. 
As some of the data failed the Shapiro–Wilk normality test, we used the 
non-parametric Friedman test to compare the three methods, showing 
that there are statistically significant differences in the number of resets 
(𝑝 = .001), the average distance between resets (𝑝 = .001), and SSQ 
scores (𝑝 = .021). The results of the post hoc analysis using the Wilcoxon 
signed-rank test with Bonferroni correction are detailed in Table  3.
As the violin plot and Table  3 show, the difference in the number 
of resets between our method and TAPF is not statistically significant. 
However, while our method performed slightly better than OPG in 
simulation environments, it showed a clear improvement in the user 
study. A possible reason is that real users do not move with perfectly 
smooth translation as in simulations. Their natural stride length may 
be too long to follow the short ‘S’-turns in the path planned by OPG, 
which results in difficulty in replicating the simulated performance. In 
contrast, our method provides more constant gains, making the path 
easier for real users to follow. On SSQ scores, our method outperforms 
the other two because its generated paths are simple, consisting of 
only a circular arc and a straight line with constant gains. In contrast, 
the curvature gain in the TAPF method changes continuously with the 
potential field, and the strategy of inserting ‘S’-turns in OPG causes 
frequent changes in the direction of the curvature gain. Such frequent 
directional changes in curvature gain likely caused user dizziness.
5.3. Discussion
One unexpected finding is that the performance of both our method 
and OPG is not as good in the user study as in the simulation. For 
comparable simulation environment C, both OPG and our method 
outperform TAPF. However, in the user study, our method does not 
show a statistically significant advantage over TAPF, while OPG per-
forms worse than TAPF. We attribute this performance drop to the 
inherent imprecision of human locomotion. Unlike simulated agents 
which follow an idealized trajectory, real users naturally produce small 
but constant deviations in their walking patterns. These errors may 
cause more frequent path replanning than in the simulation, which 
led to instability in the guidance path. Furthermore, this also triggered 
additional resets for OPG due to its reset in advance strategy.
To validate this hypothesis and quantify this behavioral difference, 
we conducted a supplementary experiment (N=8). Participants were 
asked to walk 5 m in a straight line toward a virtual target under 
five conditions: no gain, curvature gain (left/right), and strafing gain 
(left/right). We compared their final pose error (defined as the devi-
ation from a calculated ideal pose) against those of simulated avatars 
performing the same task.
As shown in Fig.  7, the position deviations of the simulated avatars’ 
are entirely within 0.05 m, while the orientation deviation was near 
0◦. In contrast, after real users walking 5 m, their position deviations 
are concentrated between 0.1 m and 0.3m — but the deviations for 
the strafing path are smaller — while the orientation deviations are 
between 0◦ and 20◦. In the previous experiment, the path deviation 
thresholds were set at 0.2 m and 10◦ to ensure accurate guidance and 
timely adjustments. The results from this supplementary experiment 
suggest to some extent that real users’ natural walking produces devi-
ations that sometimes exceed our thresholds, leading to more frequent 
path replanning.
6. Limitation and future work
There are several limitations of our method. Firstly, our user study 
was limited to the complex environment to assess algorithm perfor-
mance under challenging conditions. We did not conduct real user 
study in the obstacle-free environment, which represents a more com-
mon scenario. Future work should extend the user evaluation to include 
such open settings for a more comprehensive analysis. Another lim-
itation is that our evaluation was conducted with OpenRDW. The 
platform’s implementation of existing methods may include practi-
cal adjustments like variations in how gains are applied, which may 
not perfectly align with the methods’ original theoretical descriptions. 
However, our comparison remains valid, as all compared algorithms 
utilize the same underlying gain implementation, ensuring a consistent

Our work can be improved in the future. We observed that real 
users naturally produce small but constant deviations in their walking 
patterns, leading to walking errors and random deviations. Future 
work could involve not only designing algorithms for ideal paths, but 
also considering their performance and robustness when subjected to 
user-generated deviations. Developing adaptive strategies would be a 
viable approach to reduce the gap between simulation and real users. 
Additionally, although our method provides an initial validation for 
integrating strafing gain, its full potential remains untapped. Future 
research could aim to explore more applications for strafing gain, 
such as its deeper integration into more complex RDW controllers for 
multi-user or dynamic environments.
7. Conclusion
This paper introduced a novel RDW method that mitigates the 
increased perceptibility and user discomfort caused when state-of-the-
art trajectory planning controllers frequently switch curvature gain 
between opposite directions. Our main contribution is a novel path 
generation algorithm, integrating strafing gain with pose score guid-
ance strategy, that ensures the direction of redirection gains constant 
within each guidance maneuver. The simulation results validate that 
our approach effectively reduces the number of resets while the user 
study shows lower SSQ scores, enhancing user comfort.
